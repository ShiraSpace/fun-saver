Your three questions

Q1 · Teaching or saving?

Mostly saving and tracking.

  • Worldwide, about 30% put real weight on teaching: standalone games (Zogo, MoneyPrep, MoneyTime) or card apps with lessons (Greenlight Level Up, GoHenry Money Missions).
  • In Israel, banks and card companies have real money but almost no teaching. Hebrew teaching apps (FinanKids, Kal-Klili) keep no money ledger.
  • Nobody ties lessons to the child's own money. Goalsetter tried and closed its consumer app in Feb 2026.
Q2 · Can kids pay?

Yes. The funded players all moved to real cards.

  • Worldwide: debit or prepaid cards from Greenlight, GoHenry, BusyKid, FamZoo, Rooster, Revolut <18, Monzo, Till, Mydoh and more. Some add Apple/Google Pay and P2P.
  • Israel: Poalim Junior (8+, Google Pay 13+, Apple Pay 14+), PayBox Young (8–18, NFC and P2P), prepaid MyMAX, My Cal and Netantchik (any age), bank debit cards from 14.
  • No virtual ledger or teaching app lets a kid pay.
Q3 · Who is closest?

FamZoo abroad, FinanKids in Hebrew.

  • FamZoo: save/spend/give split, parent-paid compound interest, free virtual mode.
  • Rooster Money: free virtual pots with parent-paid interest; paid card as the upgrade.
  • FinanKids: Hebrew virtual coins with lessons, ages 7–14. No split, no giving wallet, no interest.

Chores or not

Many competitors pay kids for chores. Fun Saver deliberately doesn't.

Commission model

Money is earned. No work, no pay.

  • Built around chores: BusyKid, S'moresUp, Joon, and FinanKids in Hebrew.
  • Support both: Rooster Money, GoHenry, Greenlight, FamZoo and Homey allow a fixed allowance, paid chores, or a mix. They lead with chores in their marketing.
Fun Saver

Not tied to chores, and we recommend it stays that way.

  • Overjustification effect (Lepper et al., 1973): a promised reward weakens motivation the child already had.
  • A Fine is a Price (Gneezy & Rustichini, 2000): money attached to a social obligation replaces it. Chores become a job the child can decline.
  • What teaches is the structure: three wallets, "wait for next week", and the weekly conversation.

Weak point: Mandell's survey shows chore-based allowance scoring slightly higher on financial literacy than unconditional allowance (52.1% vs 49.1%). It's observational, and Fun Saver adds structure to the allowance, but our strongest support is the motivation research, not literacy scores.

Middle ground if parents ask: an unconditional base allowance plus optional paid "extra jobs" beyond normal family duties. It lets kids earn without paying for chores.

Gaps Fun Saver could own

  • A giving walletNo Israeli product has one, though it fits the ma'aser custom. Abroad, giving is optional or buried.
  • Interest you can watch growParent-paid interest elsewhere is weekly or monthly and shown as a number, not daily coins.
  • Ages 5 to 8Israeli banks start at 8 or 14, phone wallets at 13–14. A free, card-less ledger works with any bank.
  • Lessons from real events"Your savings just earned 3 agorot, here's why." Neither teaching apps nor money apps do this.
  • A modern virtual ledgerThe virtual apps are old or abandoned. No KYC or banking licence, and no Synapse-style partner risk.
  • No chores for payMost funded apps push paid chores. A research-backed allowance that isn't tied to chores is a clear stance, in Hebrew especially.
  • PriceCard apps cost $5–20 a month per family.

Risk: parents may eventually want a real card. Rooster Money's route, free virtual first and a paid card later, is proven. In Israel that means partnering with an issuer such as Isracard, Max or PayBox.

A card model for Fun Saver

The goal: go worldwide with cards, stay very cheap for families, and at least cover costs.

Why a free card paid by purchase fees fails in Israel: ₪15 a week of spending is about ₪780 a year per child. At Israel's capped purchase fee (about 0.3%, unverified) that earns ₪2.3 a year, before the provider's share. Per-card costs are very likely higher. Free works for US apps (1.3% fees plus float and credit) and for banks (loss leader). Fun Saver has neither.

Recommended: free tracking, card at cost plus a thin margin, one price per family

Cost levers

  • Phone-only cards by default. No printing or shipping; plastic is opt-in.
  • Charge only for active cards. An unused card costs and pays nothing.
  • Take payment on the web. About 3% instead of the app stores' 15–30%.
  • One provider, many countries. Airwallex makes a new country a settings change.
  • Price per country. Purchase fees run ~1.3% US, ~0.2% EU/UK, ~0.3% Israel, so the card could be near free in the US and ₪8–10 in Israel.

Israeli rules that matter

  • Bank account: from 14 with written parental consent, from 16 alone.
  • Cards: ATM card from 14 (₪400/day cap until 18); debit from 16, or 15 with a salary; credit from 16 with consent.
  • Prepaid cards from card companies have no minimum age; the parent holds the account. Bank prepaid products start at 8.
  • bit needs an account in the user's own name, so effectively 14+.
  • Phone wallets: Google Pay 13+, Apple Pay 14+ on a kid's Hapoalim card. Google's Wallet for kids isn't offered in Israel.
  • Greenlight, GoHenry, Current and Revolut <18 don't operate in Israel.
