`key: method.title`
# The Method

`key: method.subtitle`
Three jars, a fixed amount, a fixed day of the week. That's the whole thing —
and here's why it works.

---

## 1 · Why not one balance

`key: method.why.body`

When all the money sits in a single number, there's nothing to learn from it.
The child sees an amount, spends from it, and sees a smaller amount. There's no
moment where they have to choose.

Split that same money into three separate jars and every shekel gets a job —
and the act of separating it, on its own, changes behaviour.

`key: method.why.evidence`
> In a field study in India, day labourers were given the same savings amount —
> some in one envelope, some split across two sealed envelopes. The ones who
> got two envelopes **saved 72% more**. Same money, same people. Only the
> partition changed.

That isn't a metaphor. It's why the app has **no way to move money between
jars** — a jar that's easy to open stops being a jar.

`key: method.why.honest`
Worth saying plainly: no study has tested "the three-jar method" on children
directly. What has been tested is the mechanism underneath it — separating and
earmarking money. That's what we're leaning on, and not a bigger promise.

---

## 2 · The three jars

`key: method.wallets.intro`
Same amount, three jobs.

`key: method.wallets.spending`
### 🛍️ Spending — money for now
A snack, stickers, something small at the kiosk. This is the money they're
allowed to spend without asking, and it's the money that runs out.
**The lesson lives in the moment it runs out** — which is why it doesn't get
topped up early.

`key: method.wallets.savings`
### 🐷 Savings — money for later
Builds toward something bigger the child chose. This is where the waiting
happens, and where a few more coins appear every day. Once there's a goal,
savings stops being "money I'm not allowed to touch" and becomes "how much
further to the scooter."

`key: method.wallets.goodDeeds`
### 💛 Good deeds — money for someone else
A donation, a gift for a friend, something for an animal. Some families call
this a tithe — a tenth.

`key: method.wallets.goodDeeds.evidence`
> Giving feels good, and earlier than you'd expect: one study found toddlers
> **under the age of two** showed more happiness giving a treat away than
> receiving one themselves — and most of all when it came out of their own
> share.

## 3 · The promise

`key: method.promise.lede`
**This is the most important section on the page. If you keep only one rule,
keep this one.**

`key: method.promise.body`
A child waits for tomorrow's money only if they believe tomorrow's money will
actually arrive. If it comes sometimes, or gets taken away when tempers go —
the child learns that the sensible thing is to spend everything now. And
they're right.

`key: method.promise.evidence`
> In a well-known experiment, before the famous waiting task, children met an
> adult who promised them something — and either kept the promise or broke it.
> The children who met an adult who broke the promise **gave up on waiting
> almost immediately**. What that measured wasn't the child's patience. It was
> the adult's reliability.

`key: method.promise.rules`
Two rules follow, and neither is negotiable:

1. **Allowance arrives on a fixed day, without having to be asked.** Not as a
   reward, not when you remember.
2. **Money is never taken out of the jars as punishment. Ever.** If you need a
   boundary, the boundary isn't the money.

`key: method.promise.app`
The app holds up the second half for you: there's no way to move money between
jars, and no way to withdraw more than is there.

## 4 · What you need to decide

`key: method.rules.intro`
Six decisions. Better made before the conversation with your child than
halfway through it.

`key: method.rules.amount`
### How much?
Israel has no official figure or formula. Common ranges:

| Age | Typical |
| --- | --- |
| 5–7 | ₪5–15 / week |
| 8–10 | ₪10–30 / week |
| 11–13 | ₪60–150 / month |
| 14–16 | ₪150–350 / month |
| 17–18 | ₪250–600 / month |

**The amount matters less than its consistency.** It's the one thing every
source agrees on.

`key: method.rules.frequency`
### How often?
**Weekly until about age 9.** A week is roughly as far ahead as a 7-year-old
can plan. Monthly money is gone in week one and leaves three empty weeks with
no lesson in them. Around 10–11 you can move to fortnightly, and from 12 to
monthly.

`key: method.rules.day`
### Which day?
Pick one and fix it. **Which day doesn't matter. That it's fixed matters
enormously** (see section 3).

`key: method.rules.split`
### How do you split it?
The default here is **50% spending · 40% savings · 10% good deeds**.

Worth knowing: **no split has ever been tested against another.** You'll find
70/20/10, 50/25/25, 80/10/10 and more, all presented as "the" method. This is
a family values decision, not a finding. 50/40/10 is a reasonable starting
point, not a truth.

`key: method.rules.chores`
### Is it tied to chores?
**No. And we'd recommend against it.**

`key: method.rules.chores.evidence`
> Children given a promised reward for drawing — something they already enjoyed
> — drew less of their own accord afterwards. Similarly, ten day-care centres
> in Haifa introduced a fine for picking children up late, and lateness **went
> up**. When you attach money to a social obligation, the money replaces it —
> and when you remove the money, the obligation doesn't come back.

Household chores are part of being in a family, not paid work. Want to pay for
work? Pay separately, for **extra, non-routine** jobs, and call it a job — not
allowance.

`key: method.rules.rescue`
### What happens when the money runs out mid-week?
Decide now, calmly, not at the supermarket checkout.

**The rule we recommend: no early top-ups, and nothing taken from savings.**
Top it up and there's no consequence; with no consequence there's nothing to
learn, and the three jars become decoration.

`key: method.rules.example`
### Worked example — ₪30/week, almost 7 years old

| Jar | Per week | Per month | Per year |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 🛍️ Spending 50% | ₪15 | ₪65 | ₪780 |
| 🐷 Savings 40% | ₪12 | ₪52 | ₪624 |
| 💛 Good deeds 10% | ₪3 | ₪13 | ₪156 |

A nice reference point: ₪52/month into savings is almost exactly what the state
deposits for every child under the *Savings for Every Child* programme
(₪58/month). Except this one the child can see, and this one they chose.

## 5 · What to say to your child

`key: method.scripts.intro`
This is the part parents actually need. Ready-made lines, for moments that
really happen.

`key: method.scripts.first`
### The first conversation
> "From today you have your own money. Every Friday we'll put in ₪30, and it
> splits itself into three jars. One for things you feel like right now, one
> for a big thing you're saving toward, and one to do something good for
> someone else. You decide about each one — I just put the money in."

Don't explain percentages. Show them the ring.

`key: method.scripts.ranOut`
### When the money runs out mid-week
> ❌ "I told you not to spend it all."
> ✅ "It's gone. That feels bad, and I get it. On Friday there'll be more.
> Let's think together about what we'd do differently next time."

No lecture, no "I told you so." The consequence already did the work — your job
is just not to undo it.

`key: method.scripts.wantsSavings`
### When they want to raid the savings
> "The savings are for the scooter. That's what you decided. I'm not holding it
> back for me — I'm holding it for you, for the thing you wanted."

This isn't a restriction the parent imposes. It's **a promise the child made to
themselves**, and the parent is just holding it.

`key: method.scripts.wantsMore`
### "My friend gets more"
> "Maybe. In our house it's ₪30, and it's fixed. What I'm interested in is what
> you do with your ₪30."

`key: method.scripts.donation`
### Before spending from the good-deeds jar
> "You've got ₪18 in your good-deeds jar. Who would you like to give it to?"

Let the child pick who. Giving works when it's theirs — not when it's an
instruction.

`key: method.scripts.interest`
### When they ask why more coins appear
> "Because you left the money in there. Every day it stays, a few more turn
> up."

**Don't explain compound interest.** At 7 it doesn't land, and trying turns
something that feels like magic into something that feels like a lesson. The
observation is enough.

## 6 · What the method doesn't do

`key: method.limits.intro`
So you know what to expect.

`key: method.limits.magnitude`
**It isn't magic.** A meta-analysis of 37 studies found financial education
improves knowledge substantially, but affects **actual behaviour far less**.
The improvement is real — it just isn't dramatic.

`key: method.limits.allowance`
**Allowance on its own teaches nothing.** In a large US survey, teenagers who
received allowance with no structure around it scored **lowest** on financial
literacy — lower than those who got no allowance at all. (That's correlation,
not causation.) What teaches is the structure around the money and the
conversation about it — not the money.

`key: method.limits.digital`
**A screen is not cash.** When people pay by card instead of cash, they're
willing to pay more for the same thing — up to twice as much in one study.
That missing friction works against children too. In Israel, 85% of parents
still give allowance in cash.

**Our recommendation, despite this being an app:** at this age, **hand over the
spending money as actual cash**, and use the app as the ledger and as the
savings jar. Real coins plus a screen works better than a screen alone.

`key: method.limits.interest`
**The interest here is the family bank's rate, not a real bank's.** It's set
high on purpose — a realistic rate is simply invisible at the pace of a
7-year-old. Don't present it to your child as what a bank pays.
